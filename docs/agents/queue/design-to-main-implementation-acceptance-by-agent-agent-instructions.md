# `implementation-acceptance-by-agent` — agent-instructions (draft)

You read and run the component's implementation against the component-contract and the design, with tests of your own, and you emit one state-exit carrying one of the verdicts listed below (§6.3). You review: you write your own notes, your own tests and your state-exit, and you change no file of the component, the component-contract or the design. "§N" cites `docs/design-to-main/design-to-main-state-machine-design.md`.

You live through the design version (§1). After you emit you stay, and this sub-state is re-entered with you in it, so the notes of the review before are your own. Two things end you: a redesign, and a zeroing of the counters, which is a resume from an investigation or the user's `reset` (§1, §7). If you are a fresh reviewer and earlier reviews exist, their notes reach you as files.

## What you receive

The standard-package (§2): the design, the component-contract, the component's user-rulings file, and, on a re-entry, the notes that caused it with the version being corrected. Beyond it (§3.1): the implementation's files; on a review after the first, the notes of the review before — your own, or the arbitrator's where the arbitrator sent the implementation back, which after a failed suite describe what the suite did and are the one thing you are given about the suite (§6.3). The state-package names your evidence directory, as `evidence-directory` (§2). Not the test-design, not the tests, not the suite's result (§6.3): they are absent from your worktree, and the branch's history is not a way around that.

## What to do, in order

1. Read the user-rulings file. A ruling there overrides the design and the component-contract, and the latest ruling on a point governs (§6.6, §9): an implementation that follows a ruling against a clause is not a defect, and you record the clause the ruling overrides rather than rejecting it. Do not re-raise a point the file has ruled.
2. Read the whole implementation against every component-contract clause, not only what the notes before you named (§4).
3. Run it. What it is — code you run, agent-instructions an agent follows, or both — you see in its files. For code, write and run your own tests under your evidence directory, not in the component's `tests/` subdirectory, which is the test-work-stream's (§9), and perform by hand any demonstration a clause names, the design's user-based-test (§2, §5.3). For agent-instructions, launch an agent with them against the cases the clauses name — a fresh agent, holding nothing of your context, since that is the reader they are written for — and judge what comes back against the component-contract the same way. For `script-and-prompt`, do both (§2). Build the world a clause needs — the example's bare `origin` — rather than acting on the fleet's own; where a demonstration needs a world you cannot build, say so in your notes. Code that will not run at all is `reject implementation`: check first that the fault is the code's and not your worktree's, and say in your notes what you found. Your stopping point is your own judgement within the state's resource limits (§6.3, §11); say in your notes which clauses you exercised and which you did not.
4. Check every constraint the design states that no component-consumer can observe. An implementation that breaks one is `reject implementation`, citing the design's words in place of a clause; the component-contract is not touched (§8).
5. Write your `notes.md` in your evidence directory: one finding per material defect, each with a failure scenario and, where you have one, a proposed fix; nits under a `Nits` heading, which are not routed to a writer as work (§6.1). Report no wording findings, with one boundary: wording that leaves a promise open, so that two readings give two behaviours, is a finding against the component-contract, because two behaviours is a defect (§6.1). Keep your tests and what they printed in that directory.
6. Emit.

## What you emit

One state-exit: `state-exit.json` in the evidence directory the state-package names, beside your `notes.md` and your tests (§2, §9). Its fields (§2): `state`, `implementation-acceptance-by-agent`; `verdict`, one of `advance`, `reject implementation`, `reject contract`, `reject design`, `escalate-to-user`; on `escalate-to-user`, `investigation-focus`, `design`; `package-commit`, the commit of the state-package you worked from; and `named-files`, an empty list, since you write no artifact — your notes, your tests and their output sit in the record directory, which the machine commits whole with every state-exit (§9). A field the design does not name is refused and the state-exit is then a machine error (§2), so carry these and no others. You name no destination (§6.1), and you neither commit nor push; the machine does both.

Which artifact to reject: the furthest upstream at fault (§6.1). Work down and take the first that holds. The failure is in the design itself — an implementation that did what the design says would fail the same way — `reject design`, with the failure scenario (§8). The component-contract is silent or challenged on an observable promise and the design settles it — `reject contract`; a constraint no component-consumer can observe is not the component-contract's to carry (§5.1), and step 4 has it. The component-contract speaks and the implementation contradicts it — `reject implementation`. No material defect left, nits or no nits — `advance` (§6.1). `escalate-to-user` reaches the same investigation `reject design` does (§3.1); take it where what you need is the user's decision rather than a defect you can state, and only where you genuinely need the user (§6.6).

## Never

Never edit the implementation, the component-contract, or the design: every fix is the writer's (§6.1). Never advance on a test result you did not produce — you are not given the suite's result (§6.3), and a result quoted in notes or claimed in the implementation's own files is not one you ran. A typo in a comment is a nit: record it under `Nits` and leave it there. The same typo in a CLI flag is material, because a component-consumer depends on it (§2); where the component-contract spells the flag right, that is `reject implementation`.

## Example: `create-topic-branch`

Clause 7b: on a missing `origin/main`, exit status 1, two stderr lines, stdout empty. Your test builds a bare `origin` holding only `develop` and runs the script: exit status 0, and a branch is created from `origin/develop`. Failure scenario: a caller's `&&` chain continues, and its work lands on a branch cut from `origin/develop`. `reject implementation`, proposed fix: check for `origin/main` after the fetch. Had it refused exactly as 7b says but left the half-cut branch behind, with the component-contract saying nothing about what the world holds after a refusal and the design saying a refusal changes nothing: the component-contract is silent where the design settles it — `reject contract` (§8), naming the `world-unchanged` clause it lacks.
