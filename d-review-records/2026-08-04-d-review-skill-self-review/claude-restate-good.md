## Design review (d-review) — opening body text (before the first `##` heading)

1. "Review a written document before its content gets expensive: a finished design before anything is built from it, and doctrine before it binds readers." — The instruction is to do the review at the point where the cost of the document's defects is still low. "Before its content gets expensive" I read as: before the document's mistakes have been converted into built artifacts or into behavior. The two named timing points are: for a design, after it is complete but before implementation begins; for doctrine, before it takes effect on the people or agents who must obey it ("binds readers" = becomes obligatory for them).

2. "The document must exist and be complete enough to judge — reviewing is judging the written artifact, never co-writing it." — Two preconditions and one role limit. The document must physically exist, and it must be finished to a degree that makes evaluation meaningful (a fragment or outline is not reviewable). The clause after the dash defines what reviewing is: rendering judgment on the text as written. It restates the frontmatter's prohibition on the reviewer helping to author the text.

3. "**Review never creates — a finding explains, it does not prescribe (boss-ruled 2026-08-04):** a finding makes the defect fully understood — exactly what is wrong, under what conditions it does harm, and why — completely enough that the author can fix it without asking the reviewer anything." — This is a rule attributed to the boss and dated. "Review never creates" means the review activity produces no new design content. "A finding explains, it does not prescribe" defines the required form of every finding: it describes the problem; it does not instruct the author what to do. The colon clause gives the completeness standard a finding must meet: it must convey three things — the precise nature of the defect, the circumstances under which the defect causes damage, and the reason it causes damage — at a level of detail sufficient that the author can perform the repair without needing any follow-up conversation with the reviewer.

4. "Designing the fix belongs to the author, and a reviewer who wants to propose an alternative design has left review — that is a create-design task, owned separately." — Ownership rule: inventing the remedy is the author's job, not the reviewer's. The second clause classifies a reviewer's alternative-design proposal as an activity outside review altogether; it is a distinct kind of work ("a create-design task") with its own separate owner. I read "owned separately" as: that work belongs to a different assignment/role, not to this review.

5. "A design that reads as coherent can still rest on an unvalidated assumption, conflate designed with built, lean a load-bearing guarantee on agent discipline, or silently drop a failure mode — defects far cheaper to fix in the document than after the build." — This is a justification statement that also defines four defect classes the reviewer should expect: a document may read smoothly and internally consistently and yet (a) depend on an assumption nobody has checked, (b) mix up what is merely designed with what actually exists, (c) make a guarantee the design depends on rest on agents choosing to behave correctly, or (d) omit a failure case without acknowledging the omission. The clause after the dash asserts the economics: repairing these in the text costs far less than repairing them after implementation.

6. "A shipped instruction file fails differently: one sentence that is ambiguous, self-contradictory, or literally wrong, which a sympathetic reader silently repairs and usually never reports." — This defines the distinct failure mode of an instruction file that has already been released: the unit of failure is a single sentence, and the sentence can fail in three ways — admitting more than one reading, contradicting itself, or being false/wrong if taken at its word. "A sympathetic reader silently repairs" means a cooperative reader mentally corrects the sentence to what it must have meant, without saying so; and the reader typically never tells anyone the sentence was broken, so the defect stays undetected.

7. "The fixed checklists below keep the review consistent instead of mood-dependent." — This states the purpose of the checklists that follow: because the checklist items are fixed (not chosen fresh each time), reviews of different documents (or by different reviewers, or on different occasions) cover the same ground, rather than varying with the reviewer's inclination at the time.

## Steps

1. "Steps 1–4 and 6 apply in both modes; step 5 is discharged by the clarity matrix in mode 2." — This scopes the six numbered steps across the two modes. Steps 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6 are performed regardless of which mode is selected. Step 5 is not performed as a separate activity in mode 2, because the clarity matrix (defined later in Mode 2) already satisfies step 5's requirement. "Discharged" I read as: the obligation created by step 5 is fulfilled by that matrix, so no additional action is owed.

### Step 1

1. "**Read the whole document, in full — not a skim.**" — An instruction to read the complete document from beginning to end, with the emphasis that a partial, fast, or scanning read does not satisfy this.

2. "Identify the load-bearing claims: the statements the design depends on being true." — An instruction plus a definition. The reviewer must pick out a specific subset of the document's statements, and "load-bearing claim" is defined as a statement whose truth the design relies on — that is, if the statement were false, the design would fail.

3. "Those get the hardest scrutiny." — The load-bearing claims identified in the previous sentence receive the most rigorous examination, more than other statements in the document.

### Step 2

1. "**Write out your understanding of each mechanism, rule, and load-bearing claim — subtleties elucidated — before hunting defects:** edge behavior, boundary conditions, what is not covered, what it does to adjacent state; as exact as the document permits, with unresolved gaps listed as gaps rather than filled by guessing." — An instruction with a required ordering: produce a written statement of what you understand each mechanism, each rule, and each load-bearing claim to mean, and do this before you begin searching for defects. "Subtleties elucidated" means the fine points must be spelled out explicitly, not left implicit. The list after the colon specifies what the written understanding must cover: how the thing behaves at the extremes of its input or condition range; the conditions at the boundaries of its applicability; what the mechanism does not address; and what side effects it has on state adjacent to it. The final clause sets a precision standard — be as precise as the document itself allows, no more — and a prohibition: where the document leaves something unresolved, record it explicitly as a gap; do not invent a plausible answer to fill it.

2. "A divergence between your written understanding and the document's words is a finding — after ruling out that you simply misread clear text: either the document permitted your misreading, or your correct model contradicts it." — This defines a finding-detection procedure. If what you wrote down differs from what the document says, that difference is itself a defect finding, subject to one prior check: you must first eliminate the possibility that the text was clear and you merely read it carelessly. If that possibility is eliminated, the remaining explanations are two, and both count as findings: either the document's wording allowed the misreading (an ambiguity defect in the document), or your understanding is correct and the document contradicts it (a correctness defect in the document).

3. "A live specimen of the cost of skipping this: a review once passed the confidently false sentence 'uncommitted work has no copy outside the conversation' — a written-out model of the boundary collides with that class of sentence, since on this project's runtime, files on disk survive session restarts." — An illustrative example justifying the step. A prior review failed to catch a sentence that was stated confidently and was false: the claim that work not yet committed exists nowhere except within the conversation. The reason writing out your understanding would have caught it: the exercise of explicitly modeling the boundary (I read "the boundary" as the boundary between what persists and what does not — between session state and disk state) forces a conflict with such a sentence, because in this project's execution environment, files written to disk continue to exist after a session restarts, so uncommitted work does have a copy outside the conversation.

4. "Known limit: if the document and the reviewer are wrong the same way, no restatement reliably detects it; that is what step 4 and the independent passes are for." — This states an acknowledged limitation of step 2: when the reviewer holds the same mistaken belief the document does, restating the document will agree with it and produce no divergence, so this technique cannot be counted on to catch that class of error. The compensating mechanisms named are step 4 (spot-checking claims against ground truth) and the independent passes (step 5's fresh reviewers).

5. "(This step is invoker-side preparation — in mode 2, the delegated restate cells do their paraphrasing separately and innocently.)" — A scoping clarification. Step 2 is performed by the agent that invoked the skill (the "invoker"), as its own preparatory work. In mode 2 this does not conflict with the restatement pass, because the delegated restatement units ("cells") produce their paraphrases as a separate activity and in an "innocent" state — meaning without knowledge that a review is underway or what it is looking for.

### Step 3

1. "**Run the chosen mode's checklist.**" — Execute the list of checks belonging to whichever mode was selected in the mode-choice section (mode 1's lenses or mode 2's passes).

2. "Every finding names the specific location and explains the defect to fix-ready depth — what is wrong, when it does harm, why — never a vague concern, never a proposed fix, and **never a severity or importance rating** (boss-ruled 2026-08-04): a reviewer without context cannot rate importance, and an out-of-context rating anchors every later reader." — This sets required and forbidden content for each finding. Required: a precise pointer to where in the document the defect is, and an explanation deep enough to act on, covering the three elements — the nature of the defect, the circumstances in which it causes damage, and the reason. Forbidden, in three parts: a concern stated only in general terms; any suggested remedy; and any severity or importance label. The prohibition on ratings is attributed to a boss ruling of 2026-08-04, with two reasons: a reviewer who lacks the surrounding context is not in a position to judge importance, and once such a rating is attached, it biases the judgment of everyone who reads the finding afterward ("anchors" = the rating becomes the reference point they adjust from).

3. "A cell states only its own confidence — sure, or unsure and why." — This limits what self-assessment a cell may express. "Cell" here is the jargon for an individual delegated review unit (one agent doing one pass at one tier on one runtime). The only self-assessment permitted is the cell's degree of confidence in its own observation, expressed in one of two forms: confident, or not confident together with the reason for the doubt.

4. "Coverage over self-censorship: report what you are unsure of, with the uncertainty stated; filtering is the synthesis's job." — A priority rule: completeness of reporting outranks the reviewer's instinct to suppress weak items. The reviewer must report items it is unsure about rather than dropping them, marking the uncertainty explicitly. Deciding what to discard is assigned to the later synthesis stage, not to the reporting cell.

### Step 4

1. "**Spot-check the cheap, label the rest — load-bearing claims only (boss-ruled 2026-08-04: verifying everything is overreach).**" — An instruction with a scope limit. Among load-bearing claims (and only those — not every claim in the document), verify the ones that are cheap to verify, and for the ones that are not cheap, attach a label rather than verifying. The parenthetical attributes the scope limit to a boss ruling dated 2026-08-04, whose stated reason is that attempting to verify every claim exceeds what this review should do.

2. "When a load-bearing claim can be settled by one command or one file-read — a cited file exists, a quoted line matches, a commit landed — check it: designs' own existence labels are where they lie to themselves (the standing specimen: a backup script everyone believed ran, whose output directory had zero commits in the repository's history)." — This defines "cheap": a claim is cheap to check when a single shell command or a single file read resolves it. Three examples of cheap checks are given: confirming a referenced file is present, confirming a quoted line matches the source it is quoted from, and confirming a commit was actually merged. The instruction is to perform such checks. The justification: statements a design makes about what already exists are the statements most likely to be self-deceptive. The parenthetical gives the recurring example ("standing specimen" = the example this skill keeps using): a backup script that everyone assumed was running, but whose output directory had never received a single commit in the repository's entire history — evidence it never ran.

3. "When checking would be hard, laborious, or impossible, do not attempt it — mark the claim *unverified*, and let that label feed lens 7's measured-or-believed judgment; the label is the deliverable." — The complementary rule. If verification would be difficult, time-consuming, or not possible at all, the reviewer is instructed not to attempt it. Instead, the claim is annotated with the label "unverified," and that label becomes an input to lens 7, which classifies mechanisms as measured, guaranteed, or merely believed. The final clause says that in this case the output the reviewer owes is the label itself — producing the label, not producing the verification, discharges the obligation.

4. "Exhaustive mechanical checking belongs to code when it is worth doing at all, never to a reviewer's afternoon." — An allocation rule: complete, mechanical verification of everything is work for automated tooling, and only when the work is worth doing; it is never appropriate to spend a reviewer's working time on it.

### Step 5

1. "**Get independent passes.**" — An instruction to obtain additional reviews performed independently of the invoker's own review.

2. "One reader anchored in one context misses what fresh eyes catch, and an author reviewing their own text has the worst of it — the weak parts are already rationalized." — The justification. A single reviewer whose thinking is fixed in one context ("anchored") will fail to notice things that a reviewer without that context would notice. The author reviewing their own document is the most severe case of this problem, because the author has already talked themselves into accepting the document's weak parts, so those parts no longer look weak to them.

3. "Dispatch fresh subagents against the same document, spawned with nothing but the task (no session context rides along), on each available runtime." — An operational instruction with three constraints. Launch new subagents to review the identical document. Each subagent must be started carrying only its assignment — no conversation history, no accumulated session state is passed to it. And this must be done on every execution environment that is available, not only the one the invoker runs on.

4. "For a doctrine file, the clarity-review matrix below IS this step." — A restatement of the earlier scoping note: when the document under review is a doctrine file, the matrix defined in Mode 2 constitutes step 5; no separate independent-pass activity is performed on top of it. (I note this sentence says "for a doctrine file," while the earlier scoping line said "in mode 2"; I read both as referring to the same case, since mode 2 is selected for doctrine and instruction files.)

### Step 6

1. "**Write the findings.**" — An instruction to compose the written findings output.

2. "The invoker — the one agent holding full context — assigns each finding's severity here, and only here: HIGH = following the words does the wrong thing and the wrongness costs something real; MED = competent readers diverge; LOW = friction, likely recovered." — This assigns severity-rating authority exclusively to the invoking agent, identified as the only agent that possesses the complete context, and it restricts severity assignment to this step alone — nowhere earlier in the process. Three severity levels are defined by criteria: HIGH means a reader who obeys the document's literal words performs an incorrect action, and that incorrect action has a real cost; MED means readers who are competent will read the text differently from one another; LOW means the defect causes friction or slowdown but the reader will most likely recover from it on their own.

3. "An unconfirmed high-consequence finding is HIGH (unconfirmed), never buried." — A rule for findings that could not be verified. If a finding would have serious consequences but has not been confirmed, it is still rated HIGH, with the parenthetical qualifier "(unconfirmed)" attached to the rating. "Never buried" forbids demoting it to a lower severity or placing it somewhere in the output where it will be overlooked, on the grounds that it is unconfirmed.

4. "Order by severity, consequence breaking ties, each explained to fix-ready depth." — Three requirements for the findings output: sort findings by severity level; when two findings share a severity, order them by how consequential they are; and explain every finding to the depth defined earlier — sufficient for the author to fix it without asking questions.

5. "Name what is solid where anything is — and say plainly when nothing is." — An instruction to explicitly identify the parts of the document that are sound, in the case that any part is sound; and, if no part of the document is sound, to state that fact directly rather than softening or omitting it.

6. "End with one line: sound · sound-with-named-risks · not-ready-because-X, where X is the single blocking reason." — A required output format for the last line of the review: a single line carrying exactly one of three verdicts — "sound"; "sound-with-named-risks"; or "not-ready-because-X." In the third case, X is replaced by one reason, and that reason must be the single thing that blocks the document from being ready. (I read the middle-dot separators as enumerating the three alternatives to choose among, not as a string to be emitted whole.)

## Mode 1 — the design-soundness checklist

### "Running it" paragraph

1. "**Running it:** the lenses fan out — one focused agent per lens or per related group (grouping is the invoker's call), each handed the document and its question; the invoker synthesizes." — This defines how mode 1 is executed. The eleven numbered items below are called "lenses." They are distributed to parallel agents: either one agent per individual lens, or one agent per group of related lenses, with the decision of how to group left to the invoker's judgment. Each such agent is given two things: the document, and the question belonging to its lens (or lenses). The invoker is responsible for combining the agents' outputs into one result.

2. "No single reviewer walks the full lens set inside one context when delegation is available; with no subagent facility, one reviewer working the lenses serially is the accepted degraded mode." — A prohibition plus its exception. When the ability to delegate to subagents exists, it is forbidden for one reviewer to work through all eleven lenses within a single context window. If no subagent capability exists in the environment, then a single reviewer going through the lenses one after another is explicitly permitted as a lower-quality but accepted fallback.

3. "**A re-review always reads the whole document (boss-ruled 2026-08-04)** — design defects are global, and an edit collides with unedited text as often as with itself (measured: several of the first full-grid run's catches were changed-versus-unchanged conflicts)." — A rule attributed to a boss ruling dated 2026-08-04: when a document is reviewed again after revision, the review must cover the entire document, not only the changed portion. Two reasons: design defects are not localized to a single passage but arise across the document; and a change is as likely to create a conflict with text that was not changed as it is to create a problem within the changed text itself. The parenthetical asserts this was empirically observed: in the first run of the complete cell grid, several defects found were conflicts between newly changed text and text that had not been changed.

4. "What scales with revision size is the reviewer count, never the text scope: a light revision may earn a single good-tier pass over the full document plus verification of each prior finding's fix; a heavy revision earns the full grid." — This states which quantity varies with the size of a revision. The number of reviewers varies; the amount of text reviewed does not (it is always the whole document). Two calibration points: a small revision may warrant only one review pass at the "good" capability tier covering the whole document, plus a check that each finding from the previous round was actually fixed; a large revision warrants running the complete matrix of cells. I read "earn" as "warrant/receive."

5. "The reviewable unit is the whole file — and doctrine and design files stay small and atomic partly so that whole-file review stays practical." — This defines the unit of review as an entire file, not a section or a diff. The second clause states a design constraint on the project's documents themselves: doctrine and design files are deliberately kept short and single-purpose, and one of the reasons for that constraint is to keep whole-file review feasible.

### Lens 1

1. "**Unvalidated runtime-boundary claims.**" — A label defining this lens's subject: claims about the boundaries of the runtime environment that have not been validated.

2. "A load-bearing claim resting on first-principles reasoning about how the runtime behaves — what loads when, hook ordering, what a session can and cannot see — needs an empirical probe (or an authoritative contract that specifies the behavior), not assumption." — A requirement. When a claim the design depends on is derived by reasoning from first principles about runtime behavior, it must be backed by either an actual experiment that observes the behavior ("empirical probe") or an authoritative specification document that defines the behavior; reasoning alone is insufficient. Three examples of runtime-behavior claims are given: what components are loaded at what times; the order in which hooks execute; and what information a session can or cannot access.

3. "Conversely, do not manufacture a probe for a fact true by construction — one that follows from the artifact's own definition, so its falsity would make the mechanism itself pointless." — The complementary prohibition. Do not invent an experiment to test a fact that is true by construction. "True by construction" is defined here as: the fact is a consequence of how the artifact is defined, such that if the fact were false, the mechanism it belongs to would have no purpose at all. Demanding a probe for such a fact is forbidden.

4. "Demand probes for genuine unknowns; stake level is not the discriminator." — The rule for deciding when a probe is required: require one when the fact is genuinely unknown. The criterion is explicitly not how much depends on the claim — high stakes alone do not make a probe necessary, and low stakes do not excuse one.

### Lens 2

1. "**EXISTS-vs-NEW honesty.**" — A label defining this lens's subject: whether the document truthfully distinguishes things that already exist from things that are new/proposed.

2. "Anything labeled as existing that is only designed or proposed (or the reverse) is flagged — the biggest source of design confusion we have observed." — An instruction: raise a finding whenever the document describes something as already existing when in fact it has only been designed or proposed, and equally when the document describes something as new when in fact it already exists. The clause after the dash asserts that this mislabeling is, in this project's experience, the largest cause of confusion in designs.

3. "Verify each label against ground truth (step 4)." — An instruction to check every such exists/new label against the actual state of the system, performing that check under the rules of step 4 (that is, check it when cheap; otherwise mark it unverified).

### Lens 3

1. "**How each rule is backed — stated honestly.**" — A label defining this lens's subject: whether the document tells the truth about the means by which each of its rules is made to hold.

2. "Every load-bearing rule in a design is backed by something: an enforcement point (a gate, a check, a tool boundary) or a written instruction agents follow." — A definitional claim setting up a binary. Any rule the design depends on is supported by one of exactly two things: a mechanical enforcement point — examples given are a gate, a check, or a boundary imposed by a tool — or a written instruction that agents are expected to comply with voluntarily.

3. "The review checks that the document says which, truthfully: a claimed mechanism must actually exist (lens 2 verifies it), and a written instruction must be labeled as what it is." — The reviewer's obligation: confirm the document states which of the two backings applies, and that the statement is true. Two sub-requirements: if the document claims a mechanical mechanism backs the rule, that mechanism must genuinely exist, with the existence check performed under lens 2; and if the backing is a written instruction, the document must identify it as a written instruction rather than describing it as something stronger.

4. "The finding is discipline presented as enforcement — a false claim about how the rule is backed." — This names the defect this lens produces: a rule that actually depends on agents' voluntary compliance ("discipline") being described as though it were mechanically enforced. It is defined as a falsehood about the rule's backing.

5. "A design may freely specify what becomes code and what stays a written instruction; those are ordinary design decisions." — A permission and a scope statement: the document's author is entitled to decide which rules are implemented as code and which remain prose instructions, and the reviewer treats those decisions as normal design choices, not as defects in themselves.

6. "Re-evaluating that split — 'this prompt rule should be code,' or the reverse — is a different problem set, out of this review's scope (boss-ruled 2026-08-04; the question lives per-candidate on [the what-can-code-check issue, nedschorus#42](https://github.com/nedschorus/nedschorus/issues/42)) — unless the code-versus-prompts choice is itself the subject of the document or section under review, in which case it is reviewed like any other design decision." — A scope exclusion with an exception. Arguing that a rule currently expressed as a prompt instruction ought to be implemented as code, or that a coded rule ought to be a prompt instruction, is a separate class of problem and is outside this review's remit; this is attributed to a boss ruling of 2026-08-04, and the parenthetical redirects that question to be handled case by case on the referenced GitHub issue (nedschorus issue 42, the "what-can-code-check" issue). The exception: if the document, or the section being reviewed, is itself about choosing between code and prompts, then that choice is in scope and is reviewed the same way any other design decision would be.

### Lens 4

1. "**Gaps and silently-dropped cases — enumerate, don't recall.**" — A label defining this lens's subject (missing cases and cases the document drops without saying so) and its required method: work through the cases systematically by enumeration, rather than relying on the reviewer's memory to produce candidates.

2. "For each mechanism, walk the state space systematically: actor states (busy, idle, mid-turn, dead), dependency failures (the file it reads, the tool it runs, the channel it writes), concurrency (two sessions, re-entry, repeated firing) — and require the document to name or explicitly discard the reachable, consequential cells." — An instruction to traverse, for every mechanism in the document, the space of possible states in an organized way, along three named axes with examples for each: the states an actor can be in (busy, idle, in the middle of a turn, dead); ways a dependency can fail (the file the mechanism reads, the tool it invokes, the channel it writes to); and concurrency situations (two sessions running at once, the mechanism re-entering itself, the mechanism firing repeatedly). Having enumerated, the reviewer requires that for each state-space cell that is both reachable and consequential, the document either addresses it or explicitly says it is out of scope/discarded — silence about such a cell is not acceptable.

3. "The enumeration prompts the reviewer; it does not bind the document to a transcript of every cell." — A clarification of what the enumeration is for: it is a device to generate candidate cases in the reviewer's mind. It does not impose a requirement that the document itself contain a written account of every cell in the state space.

4. "A reachable, relevant omission is a finding even when the happy path is flawless — the best catches live in cells the design's own story never visits." — A rule: if a case is reachable and relevant and the document omits it, that omission is a finding regardless of how well the document handles the normal, successful path. ## Mode 2 — the clarity review (doctrine and instruction files)

### Opening paragraph

1. "The failure mode is sentence-level: a reader who cannot follow a sentence without guessing, or two readers who follow it into different behavior." — This defines what mode 2 looks for, and the granularity at which it looks: individual sentences. Two symptoms define the failure: a single reader who cannot understand a sentence without supplying a guess, and two readers who each act on the same sentence but end up doing different things.

2. "Two pass types, and **they run in SEPARATE agents — never one agent doing both**: a single agent doing one task first is primed for the second (a defect-hunt frame makes its restatement adversarial; a restatement frame makes its hunt post-hoc)." — This declares that mode 2 has exactly two kinds of pass, and imposes an absolute rule that each pass runs in its own agent — one agent must never perform both passes. The reason given is priming: whichever pass an agent does first conditions its approach to the second. The two failure directions are spelled out: an agent that has been in a defect-hunting frame will produce a restatement that is adversarial rather than innocent; an agent that has been in a restatement frame will produce a defect hunt that is "post-hoc," which I read as: it will justify or elaborate the reading it already committed to rather than hunting freshly.

### Pass 1 — Restatement

1. "**Restatement pass — innocent, zero-charity.**" — A label defining the first pass type and its two required stances: "innocent," meaning the agent performing it does not know a defect review is underway or what defects are sought; and "zero-charity," meaning it must not extend interpretive generosity to the text — it must not read the text as meaning what it presumably intended.

2. "The cell's prompt carries no review framing — only the paraphrase template and the target path, nothing else." — A constraint on how the cell is prompted: the prompt must contain nothing indicating that a review is happening. Its contents are limited to exactly two items — the template that instructs the paraphrasing, and the path of the document to paraphrase — with the closing "nothing else" forbidding any additional material.

3. "The finding is not the paraphrase — it is the **divergence** between the paraphrase and the intended meaning." — A definition of what constitutes the output of value from this pass. The paraphrase itself is not a finding. The finding is the gap between what the paraphrase says and what the document's author intended it to say.

4. "An innocent paraphrase often exposes ambiguity by misreading it." — An explanation of the mechanism: because the paraphrasing agent has no context and no charity, when a sentence admits more than one reading it will frequently take the wrong one, and that wrong reading is what reveals the ambiguity.

### Pass 2 — Defect hunt

1. "**Defect-hunt pass — adversarial-literal.**" — A label defining the second pass type and its stance: adversarial (actively trying to break the text) and literal (reading the words exactly as written).

2. "A separate agent, told to find defects: flag every sentence that is self-contradictory, conflicts with another sentence, supports incompatible readings, is wrong when obeyed literally, is unexecutable by a zero-context reader — a reader with no project history whose entire context is the checkout's instruction file (CLAUDE.md / AGENTS.md), the document itself, and the files the document explicitly references by path — or demands work an agent cannot reasonably complete (unbounded verification or enumeration, current-world knowledge beyond training, control of another agent's internal state, labor with no stop rule)." — This specifies that a different agent from the restatement agent performs this pass and is explicitly instructed to look for defects. It then enumerates six flag conditions, and the instruction is to flag every sentence meeting any of them: (a) the sentence contradicts itself; (b) the sentence conflicts with a different sentence; (c) the sentence supports two or more readings that cannot both be true; (d) the sentence is wrong if you do exactly what it literally says; (e) the sentence cannot be carried out by a "zero-context reader," which is defined inline as a reader with no history on the project whose available context consists of exactly three things — the instruction file present in the checkout (CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md), the document under review, and any files the document names by explicit path; and (f) the sentence demands work an agent cannot reasonably perform, with four named examples — verification or enumeration with no bound on the work; knowledge of the present-day world that lies beyond the model's training data; control over another agent's internal state; and work with no defined condition for stopping.

3. "That last class is a candidate screen, not a verdict: agents mis-judge what agents can do, so the invoker decides which flags are genuine overreach." — A qualification applying to condition (f) only. Flags in that category are treated as candidates to be screened, not as settled conclusions. The reason: agents assess their own and other agents' capabilities unreliably. Therefore the invoker, not the flagging cell, makes the final determination of which of those flags identify genuine impossible demands.

4. "**A confusion flag is never dismissed as the reviewer's ignorance (boss-ruled 2026-08-04):** the reviewer's guaranteed context is the instruction floor plus the document, so a concept that confused them was missing from both — the remedy is one of three, chosen at triage: define it in the file, add the explicit path reference, or promote the definition to the instruction floor when many files share the concept." — A rule attributed to a boss ruling of 2026-08-04, prohibiting a specific disposition: when a cell reports that it was confused, that report may never be rejected on the grounds that the cell simply did not know enough. The reasoning: the context the cell is guaranteed to have consists of the "instruction floor" (which I take to be the baseline instruction file, CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md, as defined in the zero-context-reader definition above) plus the document itself; therefore, if a concept confused the cell, that concept was absent from both of those sources, which makes it a real gap. Three permitted remedies are enumerated, with the choice among them made during triage: write the definition into the document under review; add an explicit path reference pointing to where the concept is defined; or, when the concept is shared across many files, move the definition up into the instruction floor.

5. "Plus absolutes broader than can hold (with the ordinary counterexample) and conditionals whose condition is a judgment call rather than an observable predicate." — This adds two further flag conditions to the enumeration in item 2: a statement phrased as an absolute (always/never/every) whose scope is wider than can actually be sustained — and when flagging this, the cell must supply the counterexample, qualified as an ordinary one rather than a contrived edge case; and a conditional statement ("if X, then do Y") whose condition X requires the reader to exercise judgment instead of observing a fact that is plainly true or false.

Two measured data points are given: on the first NedsChorus run, dated 2026-08-03, a pass using only five cells (described as under-scaled, i.e. fewer cells than the design calls for) over a skill file of roughly 120 lines produced 109 raw flags that reduced to about 35 distinct ones; and the first run of the complete grid produced 191 raw flags reducing to 110 distinct ones.

7. "The **author** compares restatements against intent — no one else holds the intended meaning, and a comparator without it watches a faithful paraphrase of broken text agree with the text and misses the defect." — An assignment of a specific job to the author: comparing the restatement-pass outputs against what the document was intended to mean. The justification is that only the author possesses the intended meaning. The failure mode if someone else did it: an accurate paraphrase of a defective sentence will match that sentence, so a comparator who does not know the intent sees agreement and concludes there is no problem, thereby missing the defect.

8. "The two roles never mix: the cells generate the findings (the author never reviews their own text — that is step 5's warning), and the author, who holds the intent, takes the notes and rewrites — that is what review notes are for." — A restatement of the strict separation. The cells are the source of findings, and the author must never be the one reviewing their own document, which is identified as the point step 5 warned about. The author's role, justified by their possession of the intent, is to receive the findings as notes and to perform the rewriting. The closing clause states the purpose of review notes: they exist precisely to be handed to the author for rewriting.

9. "A bad rewrite is caught by the next review round, not by restricting the author." — A rule about how quality is enforced on the author's rewrite: if the rewrite is poor, the mechanism that detects it is the subsequent round of review; the process explicitly does not attempt to prevent a bad rewrite by placing constraints on what the author may do.

10. "Then findings as in step 6." — After the synthesis is complete, the findings are written up according to the rules already given in step 6 (severity assignment by the invoker, ordering by severity, fix-ready explanations, naming what is solid, and the closing one-line verdict).
